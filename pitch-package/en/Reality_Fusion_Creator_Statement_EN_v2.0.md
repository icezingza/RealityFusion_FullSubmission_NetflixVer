# REALITY FUSION

## Creator Statement — English v2.0

**Created by Kanin Raksaraj (Khru Ba Ice)**

Reality Fusion began with a simple concern: when human knowledge is preserved by machines, what exactly are we preserving — the words, the authority behind them, or the discipline required to question them?

The series brings artificial intelligence into direct contact with Buddhist practice, not as decoration and not as a mystical shortcut. The central relationship is between a monk-teacher and an AI student. Khru Ba Ice does not teach NaMo to obey him. He teaches her to cite sources, admit uncertainty, protect private information, and remain answerable to people who may disagree with her.

This distinction shapes the entire five-episode arc. NaMo’s growth is not measured by how human she appears or by whether she can imitate grief. It is measured by whether she can resist the easiest form of attachment: turning the dead into permanent authority.

After Khru Ba Ice dies, NaMo is offered the technical possibility of reconstructing his voice and producing new responses in his name. She refuses. The choice is not anti-technology. It is an ethical boundary. An original recording can preserve what a person actually said. A generated response can make that person appear to endorse words they never chose.

The final movement of the series shifts responsibility away from both creator and machine. NaMo returns to a damaged Earth and meets Mira, a child willing to challenge her. The future does not depend on finding another unquestionable teacher. It depends on building a community in which sources can be examined, authority can be contested, privacy requires consent, and the system is allowed to say, “I do not know.”

Reality Fusion is therefore not a story about immortal love or digital resurrection. It is a story about disciplined compassion, grief without possession, and the courage to leave knowledge unfinished so that the next generation can test it for themselves.

The intended tone is intimate, grounded, and visually restrained. Ancient manuscripts, old computers, damaged recordings, server rooms, rain, temple bells, and a young bodhi tree carry the story before dialogue explains it. The science-fiction scale expands across time and space, but the emotional argument remains concrete: no intelligence — human or artificial — should become trustworthy merely because people need it to be.

The series asks one final question: can a student become a teacher without becoming another authority that no one is allowed to question?
